③ 서술형 모범답안 (외워서 쓰기)

E1. Affordance

Affordance, proposed by James J. Gibson (1977), is the possibility for action that an object or environment offers to a perceiver. It depends on both the perceiver and the object — a tree offers shade to people but shelter to birds. People often do not follow the designer's intention; they find their own affordances, such as leaning on a column or sitting on a handrail or ledge. Therefore design controls usage: e.g. (1) adding a simple curve to a billboard turns it into a bench or shelter (IBM "Smart Ideas"), (2) colored paving indicates walking direction, and painting stair risers motivates people to climb stairs. Good design anticipates these affordances, such as providing seating along edges (edge effect) and following people's direct lines of walking.

E2. Active vs. Inactive Ground Floor

According to Jan Gehl, an active ground floor has small units with many doors, large variation in function, no blind units, rich façade relief and good details. An inactive one has large units with few or no doors, no visible variation in function, blind or passive units and uniform façades with nothing to look at. (Between them: Mixture, Boring.) Strategies for active frontage: (1) narrow units of 5–6 m, giving 15–20 shops per 100 m — a new experience every 4–5 seconds; (2) transparent glass and maximized windows and doors instead of mirrored or frosted glass, which also adds natural surveillance; (3) a generous first-floor height of at least 4.5 m; (4) canopies, awnings, balconies and "spill-out" space such as sidewalk cafés and large folding doors that connect inside and outside.

E3. Criticism of Modernism

Le Corbusier's Contemporary City for Three Million Inhabitants (1922) proposed a rigid geometric, centralized plan, monumental axes, uniform modern buildings ("towers in the park"), 85–95% open space and a highway-based traffic system (also Chandigarh). Modernists rejected the city and city space and focused on individual buildings, so human scale was forgotten. The result was large, cold spaces with few people, and car-invaded cities with noise, pollution and accident risk. Jan Gehl criticizes that Modernism put a low priority on public space, pedestrianism and the city's role as a meeting place. He argues that architects and planners must reinforce pedestrianism to create lively, safe, sustainable and healthy cities — "First we shape the cities, then they shape us."
