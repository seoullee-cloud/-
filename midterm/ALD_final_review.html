⑤ 서술형 모범답안 (통 문장)

문장을 통째로 외울 필요는 없습니다. 굵게 밑줄 친 단어(이름·숫자·핵심어)는 꼭 들어가게 쓰세요.

1. Explain proxemics and personal space, and how they apply to urban design.

Proxemics is the study of how people treat their space and other people in their proximity. It was developed by the anthropologist Edward T. Hall in The Hidden Dimension (1966). A key idea is personal space, the region surrounding a person which they regard as psychologically theirs. It works like an invisible bubble, also called a body buffer zone or portable territory. When it is invaded, people feel discomfort, anger or anxiety. Personal space is not fixed; it changes with activity, intimacy, culture and circumstances. Hall described four distances: intimate (0–0.5 m) for close family, personal (0.5–1.2 m) for friends, social (1.2–3.6 m) for strangers and service providers, and public (over 3.6 m) for speeches. Experiments show that people take the end seats on the subway first and choose the urinal farthest from the door. Therefore, urban designers should respect personal space when they arrange seating, benches and the size of public spaces, so that people feel comfortable and stay longer.

2. Explain Jan Gehl's three types of outdoor activities and why city quality matters.

According to Jan Gehl in Cities for People (2010), there are three types of outdoor activities in city space. Necessary activities, such as going to work or school and waiting for the bus, take place under all conditions because people have to do them. Optional activities, such as strolling along a promenade or sitting to enjoy the view, happen only when the quality of the city is good. Most attractive city activities belong to this group. Social activities include all contact between people, from passive seeing and hearing to active contact such as greetings, conversations and children playing. When the physical quality of public space improves, people stay longer, optional activities increase, and social activities increase with them. Therefore, designers should provide good conditions for staying, such as seating along edges, sun and shade, and active ground floors, to create a lively city.

3. Explain the concept of affordance and how designers can use it.

Affordance is a concept proposed by James J. Gibson (1977). It means the possibility for action that an object or environment offers to a perceiver. Affordance depends on both the perceiver and the object; for example, a tree offers shade to people but shelter to birds. In the city, people often do not follow the designer's intention. They find their own affordances: they lean on a column while waiting, sit on a handrail or a ledge, and lie on a sloped wall. This shows that design controls usage. Designers can use affordance on purpose: adding a simple curve to a billboard turned it into a bench (IBM "Smart Ideas"), colored paving shows people which way to walk, and paintings on stair risers motivate people to climb. Good design also follows people's natural behavior, such as the edge effect, direct lines of walking and the preference for ramps over stairs.

4. Explain Kevin Lynch's concept of legibility and the five elements of the city image.

In The Image of the City (1960), Kevin Lynch introduced legibility, the apparent clarity of the cityscape. A legible city is one whose districts, landmarks and paths are easily identified and grouped into an overall pattern. Legibility is important for way-finding, gives people a sense of security, and deepens their experience of the city. An environmental image has three components: identity (recognizing an object as distinct), structure (its spatial relation to the observer and other objects) and meaning (its practical or emotional value). Lynch found five elements in people's mental image. Paths are channels along which people move, such as streets and transit lines, and they are the predominant elements. Edges are linear boundaries that are not used as paths, such as shores and walls. Districts are medium-to-large areas that people mentally enter, with a common character. Nodes are strategic points people enter, such as squares and junctions. Landmarks are point-references external to the observer, such as towers or signs. His study of Boston showed problems such as characterless paths and a hidden waterfront, so designers should create clear routes, distinct districts and recognizable landmarks.

5. Compare an active and an inactive ground floor and suggest design strategies.

Jan Gehl classifies ground floors into four levels: active, mixture, boring and inactive. An active ground floor has small units with many doors, large variation in function, no blind units, rich façade relief and good details. An inactive ground floor has large units with few or no doors, no visible variation in function, blind or passive units and uniform façades with nothing to look at. Active frontages make streets lively and safe. First, designers should use narrow units of 5–6 m, which gives 15–20 shops per 100 m and a new experience every 4–5 seconds. Second, they should use transparent glass and many windows and doors instead of mirrored or frosted glass, which also improves natural surveillance. Third, a ground floor height of at least 4.5 m gives the street level prominence. Finally, canopies, awnings, balconies and spill-out space such as sidewalk cafés and large folding doors connect the inside with the public realm.

6. Why was the Modernist vision of the city criticized?

Le Corbusier's Contemporary City for Three Million Inhabitants (1922) represents the Modernist vision. It had a rigid geometric plan, monumental axes, uniform high-rise buildings standing in 85–95% open space ("towers in the park"), and a highway-based traffic system; Chandigarh is a built example. Modernists rejected the city and city space and focused on individual buildings, so human scale was forgotten. The result was large, cold spaces with few people and car-invaded cities with noise, pollution and accident risk. Jan Gehl criticizes that Modernism put a low priority on public space, pedestrianism and the role of city space as a meeting place. He argues that architects and planners must reinforce pedestrianism to create lively, safe, sustainable and healthy cities, because "first we shape the cities, then they shape us."

7. Explain the seven principles of urban design.

Urban design is the process of making better places for people than would otherwise be produced. The Urban Design Compendium (Llewelyn-Davies) gives seven principles. Places for People means places must be safe, comfortable, varied and attractive, with good quality public space and continuity and enclosure. Enrich the Existing means new development should respond to local character, history and storytelling. Make Connections means places must be easy to reach and move through, supporting ease of movement and legibility. Work with the Landscape means using the site's climate, landform and ecology to balance the natural and built environment. Mix Uses and Forms creates diversity and choice, as in Roppongi Hills. Manage the Investment means projects must be economically viable and well maintained to keep sustainable land value. Design for Change means development should be flexible to respond to future needs, which is adaptability, as in Hammarby Sjöstad.

8. How can we study public life? Explain the questions and methods.

Jan Gehl and Birgitte Svarre (How to Study Public Life) suggest five basic questions. How many people are moving or staying is the most basic question and gives quantitative data for before-and-after comparison. Who asks about gender and age, which can be observed, while job or income needs a questionnaire. Where asks where people walk and stay, for example along the edges. What maps necessary, optional and social activities. How long measures walking speed and staying time, because people walk slower and stay longer in good places. The field methods are counting, mapping (marking where people stay at a moment), tracing (drawing lines of movement), tracking (following people with a stop-watch), photographing, keeping a diary and test walks. The results are shown in an existing conditions diagram, with a scale, north arrow and legend, and in an analysis diagram of flows and conflicts.

9. Why do streets matter, and what makes a great street?

Streets make up a large part of city-owned land and are the most important public spaces in a city. As Jane Jacobs said, "if a city's streets look interesting, the city looks interesting." Streets have community functions, as ceremonial spaces, social spaces, places of commerce and outdoor rooms, and transportation functions for transit, vehicles, bicycles, walking and parking. A street has three realms: the private, pedestrian and roadway realms. Pedestrian-friendly streets follow the 5 C's: connective, convenient (no more than 10 seconds waiting to cross), cheerful, comfortable and clear. Traffic calming, such as curb extensions and mid-block narrowing, reduces car speed, and street trees and bioswales make streets work as ecosystems. Great streets are accessible, attractive, safe and comfortable, and they support economic vitality and community life.
